## Reputation, Ranks and the Single Shogun Seat

The advertised ceiling is one Shogun, which changes how you should think about progression. In a game where everyone can become a lord, rank is a personal milestone. Here, authority is competitive, so reputation likely functions as social currency as much as a stat bar. That means visible behavior — fulfilling work, honoring deals, showing up where you said you would — may matter as much as any hidden number. Roleplay etiquette is player practice rather than a documented mechanic, but in a competitive hierarchy it is also practical strategy: a character known for reliability gets invited into the groups that actually contend for power.

Players commonly report that social friction is the real difficulty curve, since a lord with a bad reputation loses the cooperation that wealth depends on. Treat that as community observation, not a rule. The clean way to confirm how reputation behaves is to check the official page for updates, then watch whether NPCs, job boards or other players respond differently as your standing changes.

## Roleplaying in Edo-Period Japan

The setting invites period-appropriate interaction, and most servers reward it. Keep your character's speech consistent with the era, bow when greeting someone of higher standing, and avoid modern slang unless the room is clearly casual. Ask before initiating combat or dramatic scenes, and use in-game chat or the game's own communication tools rather than external voice calls with strangers.

Finding a role is usually a matter of presence. Idle near a town center, offer labor to an established player, or ask in chat what work is needed. This is player practice, not documented design, but it matches how competitive hierarchy games tend to operate. If you want a specific role, ask an active group directly instead of waiting to be recruited.
